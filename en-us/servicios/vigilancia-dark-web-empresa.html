Your company's passwords may already be for sale — through an old phishing attack, a hacked third-party site or an infected machine. The question isn't whether they leak, but who finds out first: you, or whoever buys them.

The problem

Most break-ins aren't "hacked." They're logged into.

When a third-party site suffers a breach, the passwords your team reused end up in dumps that are bought and sold. Credential thieves (infostealers) do the same with infected machines: they steal every password saved in the browser and post them on criminal marketplaces.

With a valid credential, the attacker doesn't need to break anything: they get into your email, read, wait and run the fraud at the perfect moment. Catching the leak in time — and changing that password today — cuts the chain before the incident.

Frequently asked questions

Before you sign up

What exactly is the dark web, and why should I care?

It's the part of the internet where stolen credentials, corporate access and leaked data are bought and sold. If one of your team's passwords shows up there, an attacker can walk into your email or your Microsoft 365 without breaking anything: just by logging in.

What happens when you find something?

You get an alert with context and a concrete action plan: which account to change, which sessions to close, what to review. We don't send scares without instructions.

Does this replace a security audit?

No, it complements it. Monitoring warns you about what has already leaked; the Q-Start diagnosis fixes the doors it leaks through. The usual approach is to start with monitoring and, once the first findings come in, run the audit.
